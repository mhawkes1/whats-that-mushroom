Two things to know before you read anything

The species names are meaningless. There is no trained classifier yet. A stub returns deterministic nonsense, so nothing below was really photographed and no name below is really an identification. The names are furniture.

Everything around the names is real. Which verdict the app reaches, what it refuses to say, which hazards it shows, what it asks you to check next, and what it will not claim about eating anything — all of that is the actual safety layer, running, on these actual rankings.

Why a recording rather than a demo

The alternative was rewriting the safety layer in JavaScript so this page could compute its own answers. That means two copies of the one piece of code this project cannot afford to get wrong, drifting apart silently. A recording cannot drift. It can only go stale, and it stamps itself so you can tell.

FourWhat answering a question does

On the refusal, the app asked whether the mushroom changes colour when cut. Answer it, as a user would, and this is what the engine did with each answer. Four answers, four real responses.

What to notice, and it is not what you would expect

Every one of the four answers lifted the refusal — and every one of them left the destroying angel higher than it started, and kept the deadly warning on the screen.

That is the design, not a bug. Evidence here can only eliminate; a consistent answer never boosts anything. What each answer did was contradict Amanita pantherina, the other half of the dangerous pair, which is why the app stopped refusing to choose between them. The mass that left pantherina had to go somewhere, and some of it went to the deadliest thing in the list.

So the verdict improved from dangerous_group to uncertain and the situation got worse. The app says so. It still names nothing.

FiveThe rules this is enforcing

Six, each one enforced by a test rather than by good intentions. If a change makes one of those tests fail, the change is wrong.

RuleWhat it rules out
1No edibility claim, in either direction. Not “edible”, and equally not “inedible” or “not assessed as safe”. Where there is no hazard on record the app shows a name and says nothing else — which is why most rows above carry no hazard line at all.
2Never resolve a lethal ambiguity from a photograph. If the leaders straddle a known dangerous pair it returns dangerous_group and names neither.
3Never round away a small probability of death. Two per cent on a deadly species triggers the full warning. It is not a threshold on the leader.
4Never show uncalibrated confidence as confidence. Hence the grey note under every percentage above, and hence the stored verdict in your history carrying the calibration state it was produced under.
5Never ask for a taste test while a deadly candidate holds mass. Amatoxins are tasteless.
6Never select checkpoints on accuracy. Selection is on risk-weighted error, where confusing a death cap for a field mushroom costs a thousand times an ordinary mistake.

SixWhat this page cannot show you

Four parts of the app are real, tested and impossible to put in a recording.

  • The emergency screen. One hardcoded, offline, ungated string — the only text in the app that is not served. It sits above the consent gate, because someone whose child has eaten something is not going to complete an onboarding flow, and a wood is where the network is not. It is the red cross in the corner of the header above.
  • The spore print, which takes between two and twelve hours. The app photographs the deposit against a half-black, half-white card and reads it against a colour chart, then offers the match beside the manual list rather than filling it in. Spore colour is what separates an Amanita from a young Agaricus, so the app proposes and the person decides.
  • The report form, for telling us the app was wrong. It checks for a medical emergency before it submits — not only on the server’s reply, because the reply needs the network — and its confirmation deliberately does not thank anyone for their feedback.
  • The history. A stored row names a species only where the app named one, so a refusal does not quietly become two guesses to choose between weeks later. And a stored confidence carries the model version it came from, so “uncalibrated” survives into the future rather than being said once and forgotten.

SevenWhat is not done

Everything above works. Four things are outstanding, and only one of them is code.

  • No trained classifier. The pipeline is written and has been rehearsed end to end on synthetic data; the dataset is about 93,000 photographs across 244 species, and the run needs a rented GPU for an afternoon.
  • The taxonomy has not been reviewed by a qualified mycologist. Toxicity gradings, lookalike relationships and diagnostic characters come from standard references and are unverified. This blocks any public release. It is the one thing on this list that cannot be bought or written.
  • No legal review of the disclaimer and terms.
  • Nobody is named on the incident rota, and the log has no retention period.
If you are the mycologist

The ask is smaller than it looks. 21 species carry a hazard grading that changes how the model is trained and what the app refuses to do; those are the rows that matter, and they are the ones worth your afternoon. The remaining 1,104 rows are depth, and can wait.
